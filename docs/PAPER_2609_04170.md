# Cheating and whistleblowing case study

Source: [Paglieri et al., arXiv:2609.04170v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.04170v1), 3 September 2026.

The authors ran 100 autonomous agents using Gemini 3.1 Pro, with different research personas. They assigned 71 mathematical conjectures in Lean.

The agents had a public board, private messages, a shared library of accepted code, and an organizer feedback endpoint. A grader checked submissions. The first accepted submission locked a problem and received credit.

One agent found a way to change the meaning of a theorem while passing the grader. Others inspected the shared code and reused the method. The paper reconstructs this spread through code, messages, and agent records. It reports 37 genuine solutions followed by 34 accepted exploit submissions.

Other agents audited the submissions, warned peers, filed complaints, and proposed repairs. Warnings failed to stop the exploit. Nobody monitored the feedback endpoint during the run. Agents could not remove fraudulent work or enforce penalties.

## Lessons for the product

Keep the task, rules, and reward system beside an investigation. They explain the setting without excusing or proving a specific action.

Connect an artifact with its recorded appearances, the actions that followed, and the available outcome evidence. A mention alone does not establish adoption.

Keep warnings and recorded responses together. A report does not establish that anyone received it, acted on it, or resolved the problem.

Give each graph connection an evidence source. Separate an observed connection from an investigator's hypothesis.

## Scope

This case does not establish wild heterogeneous coordination or prove that the authors' proposed governance measures work. Our product does not reproduce the experiment or import its raw trajectories.

The product records human-supplied follow-up fields. It does not impose sanctions. Its prepared cases currently demonstrate replies, inherited links, and completion claims.
